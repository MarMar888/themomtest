# The Mom Test — Master Reference Document
*Rob Fitzpatrick | Customer Discovery & Interview Framework*

---

## What Is The Mom Test?

The Mom Test is a set of rules for asking questions during customer interviews such that even a loving, biased mother cannot give you misleading answers. Named after the idea that "even your mom will lie to protect your feelings," the method forces conversations onto objective, verifiable territory — past behavior, concrete facts, real spending — instead of hypotheticals and opinions.

**Core premise:** You shouldn't ask *anyone* whether your business idea is a good idea. It's a bad question and everyone will lie to you, at least a little. It's not their responsibility to tell you the truth. It's yours to find it.

**The real enemy:** False positives. Bad data is more dangerous than no data. Misleading signals of validation send founders down expensive wrong paths with false confidence.

## The 3 Core Rules

### Rule 1: Talk About Their Life, Not Your Idea
- Focus entirely on the customer's context, workflow, experiences, and pain points
- The moment you pitch your idea, people shift from honest observers to polite supporters
- "Once you start talking about your idea, they stop talking about their problems."
- Avoid mentioning your idea for as long as possible — ideally never in early-stage conversations
- *Easiest improvement you can make:* just don't mention your idea, and you'll automatically ask better questions

### Rule 2: Ask About Specifics in the Past, Not Generics or Opinions About the Future
- Past behavior is real data. Future intentions are over-optimistic lies
- "What did you do last time this happened?" > "What would you do if...?"
- "How have you tried to solve this?" > "Would you use a product that did X?"
- People always describe who they *want* to be, not who they *are*
- Hypothetical answers ("I'd definitely use that!") are worthless — they cost nothing to give

### Rule 3: Talk Less, Listen More
- The more you're talking, the worse you're doing
- Your job is to extract signal, not deliver a pitch
- Silence and follow-up questions ("why?", "tell me more about that") are your most powerful tools
- Let them go on tangents — the most revealing insights often come from seemingly off-topic stories

## The 3 Types of Bad Data

Every conversation is full of data that *feels* useful but isn't. Learn to recognize and deflect:

### 1. Compliments
- "That's such a great idea!" / "I love this!" / "You're really onto something."
- Compliments cost the customer nothing and feel good to receive — which makes them dangerous
- They shift the conversation's focus to *you and your idea*, away from *them and their problem*
- **Compliments are the fool's gold of customer learning: shiny, distracting, and worthless.**
- How to handle: Deflect and anchor back. "I'm glad it resonates — tell me about the last time you ran into this problem."

### 2. Fluff (Generics, Hypotheticals, and the Future)
- "Oh yeah, I do that all the time." / "I would definitely pay for something like that."
- Vague, unanchored statements with no specific evidence behind them
- Fluff is *salvageable* — you can tie it down to something concrete
- How to handle: Ask for a specific instance. "When was the last time that happened? Walk me through it."

### 3. Ideas (Feature Requests & Wishlists)
- "You should add X!" / "It would be amazing if it could do Y."
- Don't implement feature requests — understand the *motivation* behind them
- Ideas and feature requests should be understood, not obeyed
- Digging into a request reveals the real problem. The stated solution is often not the actual need
- Classic example: Customer asked for analytics → really just wanted a weekly PDF to show their clients. The flexible dashboard that was built was entirely unnecessary.
- **Questions to dig into feature requests:**
  - "Why do you want that?"
  - "What would that let you do?"
  - "How are you coping without it?"
  - "How would that fit into your day?"

## The Commitment & Advancement Framework

Conversations that end only in compliments are failed conversations. Real validation requires the customer to give up something they value.

### Two Desired Outcomes from Every Meeting
- **Commitment** — They are showing they're serious by giving up something they value: time, reputation, or money
- **Advancement** — They are moving to the next step of your real-world funnel, getting closer to a sale

### The 3 Currencies of Commitment (escalating weight)

**Time**
- Clear next meeting with known goals
- Sitting down to give feedback on wireframes/prototypes
- Using a trial for a non-trivial period
- *Lightest form — if they won't give 30 more minutes, that's a clear signal*

**Reputation Risk**
- Intro to peers or their team
- Intro to a decision-maker (boss, spouse, lawyer, budget-holder)
- Giving a public testimonial or agreeing to be a case study
- *Heavy — putting their name and credibility on the line*

**Money**
- Letter of intent (non-legal but meaningful agreement)
- Pre-order or deposit
- Actual payment
- *"I'd definitely pay for that" means nothing. "Here's $500, let me know when it's ready" means everything.*

### The Commitment Spectrum (signals to read)
From weakest to strongest:
1. "Sounds cool, keep me posted" → **Zombie lead. Not real.**
2. "Send me more info when it's done" → Weak. No skin in the game.
3. Agrees to another meeting with specific agenda → Mild commitment
4. Gives you intro to decision-maker → Real skin in the game
5. Signs letter of intent / puts down deposit → Serious signal
6. Pays money → The only truly reliable signal

> "A meeting has succeeded when it ends with a commitment to advance to the next step."
> "It's not a real lead until you've given them a concrete chance to reject you."
> "Customers who keep being friendly but aren't ever going to buy are a particularly dangerous source of mixed signals."

**If you don't know what happens next after a meeting, the meeting was pointless.**

## Question Design: Good vs. Bad

### Bad Question Patterns
| Bad Question | Why It's Bad |
|---|---|
| "Do you think this is a good idea?" | Asks for opinion, invites polite lie |
| "Would you pay for something that did X?" | Hypothetical, future-oriented, over-optimistic |
| "How much would you pay for this?" | Hypothetical pricing; people lie |
| "Do you ever have trouble with Y?" | Leading, binary, invites yes |
| "Would you use a product like this?" | Hypothetical future behavior |

### Good Question Patterns
| Good Question | Why It Works |
|---|---|
| "Tell me about the last time you ran into this problem." | Past, specific, narrative |
| "How are you dealing with this today?" | Reveals current behavior and workarounds |
| "What have you already tried to fix this?" | Reveals problem severity and prior investment |
| "How much did that cost you / how long did it take?" | Anchors to real data |
| "Who else gets involved when this happens?" | Reveals decision-making structure |
| "What are the implications when this goes wrong?" | Distinguishes real pain from minor annoyance |
| "Walk me through what you did the last time this came up." | Forces specificity, reveals actual workflow |

### The "Have They Searched for a Solution" Test
- If they haven't already looked for ways to solve this problem, they're not going to look for (or buy) yours
- This is one of the strongest early signals of whether a problem is real and urgent
- Ask: "Have you ever googled this or looked for tools to handle it?" No = major red flag

## Signals: What to Listen For

### Strong Positive Signals
- They describe a specific, recent, painful instance of the problem with emotion
- They've already built a workaround (even a messy one) — they're paying the pain tax
- They've spent money trying to solve it (even in indirect ways)
- They actively searched for solutions
- They volunteer to introduce you to others with the same problem
- They ask "when can I buy this?" or "how do I get early access?"
- Emotional words: frustration, embarrassment, costly, it kills us, we lose hours

### Weak / Misleading Signals (Red Flags)
- Pure enthusiasm without any specific story behind it ("wow, that sounds amazing!")
- They've never tried to solve it and don't seem bothered
- Vague frequency language ("yeah, I deal with that sometimes")
- Future-tense commitments ("I'd definitely try it when it's ready")
- "Let me know when it launches" → classic zombie response
- They suggest many features but show no urgency to actually get it
- **"Meh" is more useful than "wow"** — lukewarm responses tell you something real; excitement can be cheap

### Emotional Signals During the Conversation
- Sighs, eye rolls, hesitations → genuine pain points
- Leaning forward, speeding up speech → topic that matters
- Quick dismissal → not actually important to them
- "That's actually something we struggle with a lot" unprompted → gold

## Customer Segmentation

### Why Segmentation Matters
- Talking to too many types of people gives you confusing, contradictory signals
- Don't have one conversation each with 20 different customer types — have 20 conversations with 1 type
- If you aren't finding consistent problems and goals, your customer segment is too broad

### Good Segment Criteria
- A good customer segment is a **who + where pair**
  - *Who:* specific type of person (role, behavior, context)
  - *Where:* specific place you can actually find them
- If you don't know where to find your customers, keep slicing your segment into smaller pieces until you do
- **"Students" is too broad. "Junior marketing managers at mid-size e-commerce companies who manage paid ad budgets alone" is a segment.**
- Before serving everyone, you have to serve someone

### Early Adopter Characteristics (your ideal early customer)
1. Has the problem
2. *Knows* they have the problem
3. Has budget to solve the problem
4. Has already cobbled together a makeshift solution
5. Is actively seeking something better

### The 3 Segmentation Traps
1. Too broad — dilutes signals, can't find them
2. Too narrow too early — before you have evidence to narrow
3. Mixing multiple segments — results in contradictory requirements

## The "Big 3" Questions System

### What It Is
Before every batch of conversations, define the 3 most important things you need to learn. These should be questions that, if answered wrongly, would destroy your current business model or assumption.

### How to Use It
- Write them down before each conversation
- Every question you ask should connect back to one of the Big 3
- Update the list as your assumptions evolve
- **If you don't know what you're trying to learn, don't have the conversation**

### Anatomy of a Good "Big 3" Question
1. **Frequency** — How often does this problem occur? (signals market size and urgency)
2. **Spending** — What have they already invested in solving this? (signals willingness to pay)
3. **Urgency** — Are they actively seeking solutions or just tolerating the problem? (signals whether a market exists)

### The Destroy-Your-Business Question
> "Every time you talk to someone, you should be asking one question which has the potential to destroy your currently imagined business."

This is the most important discipline. You are looking for disconfirmation, not validation. The question should be the one you're most afraid to ask.

## Running the Conversation

### Before the Conversation
1. Choose a focused, findable segment
2. Decide your Big 3 learning goals with your team
3. Decide what ideal commitment/advancement looks like at the end
4. Prepare your opening framing (see below)

### The 5-Part Meeting Opening (to set the right tone)
1. **You are** — your role/who you are
2. **You're working on** — describe the problem space, not your solution
3. **You want to learn** — be honest about why you want to talk to them
4. **You'd like** — specifically what you're asking for (15 min of their time)
5. **Is that okay?** — make it easy to say no; this filters out people who'll waste your time

### During the Conversation
- Take control early — don't let them flip it into a pitch session at you
- Reiterate your purpose, then go to your first question
- When they compliment → deflect and anchor to specifics
- When they give fluff → ask for a specific example from the past
- When they give a feature idea → dig into motivation, not the idea itself
- When they go on a tangent → let them; it's often revealing
- Take notes on paper (typing comes across as rude); capture exact quotes when possible
- **Two interviewers max** — one to talk, one to take notes; three is overwhelming

### After the Conversation
- Transfer notes to a shared system (Google Docs, spreadsheet) immediately
- Debrief with your team — discuss key quotes and takeaways
- Review at the meta-level: what patterns are emerging across conversations?
- Update your Big 3 questions based on what you learned
- Archive important findings for future product design reference
- Update your action plan

### Note-Taking Guidance
- Capture **exact quotes** wherever possible — they're more valuable than summaries
- Tag signals by type: Problem / Behavior / Workaround / Emotion / Commitment / Red Flag
- Spreadsheets work well: put key signals in their own column for sorting
- Notes are useless if you don't look at them

## How Many Conversations to Have

- Keep having conversations until you stop hearing new things
- If you're still getting consistent new information after 10+ conversations, your segment is still too broad
- For a well-defined segment, patterns typically emerge after 5–10 conversations
- Don't over-prepare for interviews, and don't endlessly interview without acting
- Spend a few weeks doing interviews, get your bearings, then build something to show

## Format & Setting

### Keep It Casual, Not Formal
- Learning works better as a quick, casual chat than a long formal meeting
- If it feels like they're doing you a favor, it's probably too formal
- Coffee conversations, hallway chats, and short calls often yield better data than scheduled "customer interviews"
- 15–30 minutes is usually enough; you don't need more than 15 minutes if your questions are sharp

### Finding People to Talk To
- Warm introductions are far better than cold outreach
- Once you get one conversation, ask for intros to others in the same segment — this is a commitment signal and grows your pipeline
- Leverage communities, events, forums where your segment naturally gathers
- If you can't find where your segment hangs out, your segment definition is still too broad

### Keeping the Idea Hidden (Early Stage)
- "Give as little information as possible about your idea while still nudging the discussion in a useful direction."
- You can discuss the problem space without ever mentioning your solution
- If it's a topic you both care about, you can have an extremely valuable conversation without your idea ever entering the equation

## Interpreting Signals: A Decision Framework

### Is This Problem Worth Solving?
Ask these questions after each conversation:

| Question | Green Signal | Red Flag |
|---|---|---|
| Do they have a specific recent story about this problem? | Yes, emotional and detailed | No, or very vague |
| Have they looked for solutions? | Yes, actively | Never searched |
| Have they spent money/time on workarounds? | Yes | No workarounds attempted |
| What's the implication when this problem occurs? | Costly (time, money, reputation) | Mildly annoying at worst |
| How often does it happen? | Frequently | Rarely |
| Did they commit to anything at the end? | Yes — time, intro, or money | Just "sounds cool!" |

### Is Your Segment Right?
- Consistent problems across 5+ conversations → segment is probably right
- Contradictory problems → segment is too broad, or you're mixing types
- Nobody cares much → either wrong segment or wrong problem

## The Validation Lifecycle

### Stage 1: Problem Validation (Early — Before Building Anything)
- Goal: Confirm the problem is real, frequent, and painful
- Method: Pure Mom Test conversations, no idea mention
- Success signal: Consistent stories of pain, workarounds, and prior solution-seeking
- What you're NOT trying to do: Sell anything, validate your solution

### Stage 2: Solution Validation (When You Have Something to Show)
- Goal: Confirm your approach to solving the problem resonates
- Method: Show prototypes/wireframes, ask for feedback and commitment
- Now you can mention your idea — but ask for commitments, not opinions
- Watch for: Excitement without commitment (not a signal)

### Stage 3: Early Sales (When You Have a Product)
- Goal: Revenue and repeatable sales process
- In early-stage sales, the real goal is *learning*. Revenue is a side effect.
- Treat every failed sale as a learning conversation
- "People stop lying when you ask them for money."

## Common Mistakes & How to Avoid Them

| Mistake | Why It Happens | How to Fix It |
|---|---|---|
| Seeking validation instead of truth | Ego protection | Actively look for disconfirmation |
| Pitching before learning | Excitement | Commit to zero-pitch first conversations |
| Asking opinion questions | Feels natural | Rewrite every question to be past-tense and specific |
| Accepting fluff at face value | Conversations flow | Always ask "can you tell me about a specific time?" |
| Ending without a commitment ask | Fear of rejection | Prepare your desired next step before the meeting |
| Not transferring notes | Laziness / busyness | Transfer within 24 hours while memory is fresh |
| Bottlenecking learning in one person | Efficiency | Involve the whole founding team in customer conversations |
| Using "Customer told me so" to drive features | Misuse of process | Ask why they want it, dig to motivation |
| Celebrating compliments | Feels good | Actively remind yourself compliments are worthless |
| Talking to too many different types | Feels like progress | Commit to one segment at a time |

## For This Project: Application Notes

This project uses The Mom Test in two ways:

### 1. Evaluating How Well We Performed the Mom Test
When reviewing conversation transcripts, assess:
- Did we ask past-specific questions or hypothetical/opinion questions?
- Did we pitch our idea, or keep the focus on their life?
- Did we accept compliments and fluff, or did we dig in?
- Did we talk too much or listen well?
- Did we uncover specific stories of pain, or just get enthusiasm?
- Did we ask for a commitment at the end?
- Did we ask the "destroy your business" question?

### 2. Digesting Customer Answers into Insights
When analyzing what customers said:
- Separate responses into: Real Data (specific past stories, behaviors, spending) vs. Bad Data (compliments, fluff, ideas)
- Look for the underlying motivation behind any feature requests
- Flag emotional signals (frustration, embarrassment, urgency)
- Note what workarounds they've already built — this is evidence of real pain
- Note what they've spent money on — this is the closest signal to willingness to pay
- Track consistency across conversations — patterns = real; one-offs = noise
- Identify what was NOT said — what problems did they *not* mention that you expected?

⏺ Pre-Product Sales Commitments Are Now Situational

  The traditional logic for pre-product commitments went like this: building is expensive, so before you build, get someone to commit. The commitment was a gate that filtered out bad bets    
  before you sank months into them. It made sense when a prototype cost $500K and six months.
                                                                                                                                                                                               
  When building a prototype costs two weeks, the math breaks.                                                                                                                                  
  
  The old calculus:                                                                                                                                                                            
  Getting a pre-product commitment is itself costly — you're asking someone to evaluate an abstraction, which requires trust, relationship, and multiple conversations. If that process takes
  three weeks and building a prototype takes twelve, the commitment is clearly worth pursuing first. The cost of the commitment conversation is small relative to the cost of building wrong.  
  
  The new calculus:                                                                                                                                                                            
  If the prototype takes two weeks, the commitment conversation might cost more in elapsed time than just building. Worse, an abstract description is a poor communication medium — customers
  often can't accurately assess whether they want something they've never seen. You end up with soft commitments that evaporate on first contact with the actual product, or you over-specify  
  up front to compensate and lose speed.
                                                                                                                                                                                               
  So the prototype becomes the discovery artifact. Instead of: discovery → commitment → build → show, the sequence compresses to: discovery → prototype → show → then commitment.              
  
  Where pre-product commitments still make sense:                                                                                                                                              
  - The build requires irreversible choices that only the customer can specify (deep integrations, regulated data, proprietary systems)                                                      
  - The customer's willingness to commit is itself signal about market urgency — you're testing demand, not just communicating the product                                                     
  - Enterprise deals where trust is the bottleneck and a prototype signals unseriousness rather than competence                           
  - Co-development arrangements where the commitment funds the build                                                                                                                           
                                                                                                                                                                                               
  Where jumping to prototype is now correct:                                                                                                                                                   
  - The product is experiential and abstract descriptions produce unreliable signal ("I'd use that" means nothing until they try it)                                                           
  - The customer segment is time-constrained and won't engage with hypotheticals                                                                                                               
  - You're early enough that you need to discover the right problem, not validate a known one — a prototype surfaces that faster than conversation                                           
                                                                                                                                                                                               
  The key shift is that commitment-first was a heuristic for managing build cost, not a first-principles rule about customer learning. Now that the heuristic's underlying assumption has      
  changed, the situations where it applies have narrowed.
