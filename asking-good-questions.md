Asking Important Questions
To really help your business, it’s not enough for your customer learning to simply be “unbiased.”

Good customer learning must be:

Unbiased (it’s trustworthy)

Important (it’s strategically relevant)

Complete (it gives a full picture, not a half-truth)

Completeness can’t be achieved by asking a single perfect question, but is rather gained (or lost) by how you follow up and respond to the conversation as it happens. Unlike bias, which you ideally want to avoid, incomplete answers simply need to be finished.

Importance depends on what you do ahead of time while planning your learning goals (topics), and also on your ability to repeatedly steer the conversation back toward those topics.

We’ll cover importance first, and then loop back to completeness, covering our three remaining conversational tools along the way:

Bad Data via Omission	Sounds like	Primary tool
Off-topic	(solid insights about irrelevant topics)	Reset and refocus
Early endings	(self-conscious about talking too much)	Permission to continue
Generalizations	“I always / never / usually”	Explore edge cases
Begin by Building a Mental Model
As companies grow, their situations become more unique and their learning goals diverge. But early on, pretty much every new business wants to fill in the blanks on the same set of fairly existential learning goals:

What they’re already doing and why:

How are they currently handling this issue? (even if that means ignoring it)

Why are they doing it that way?

How does that fit within the rest of their day / work / year / life?

[if it’s unsolved] why haven’t they solved it? How often does it occur? Does it really matter?

What they’re not doing and why not:

What else did they try before settling on their current approach?

Why didn’t those things work out?

What potential solutions did they not even bother trying? (and why not?)

What made those alternatives “worse,” to their eyes, than their current solution?

How they make decisions:

How frequently and actively do they search for alternatives / improvements?

How do they discover, evaluate, and decide on these solutions?

What stands to be lost or gained? (and do they actually care, relative to other priorities?)

[if selling to businesses or governments] who are the other stakeholders, decision-makers, partners, beneficiaries, and anyone else who could silently torpedo purchase or adoption?

☝️ Remember: these learning goals are what you want to know, not how you’re going to ask. Each is a topic or a stage of the conversation where you’ll linger. You won’t work through it like a script; rather, you’ll pause on a learning goal and ask multiple questions in multiple ways—collecting concrete stories of their existing behavior—until you’ve got a clear understanding of that part of the customer’s life.

Taken together, they give you a mental model of the customer’s current behavior, worldview, and decision-making process. Equipped with this mental model, you’ll find yourself able to run a reasonable simulation of your customers’ likely reactions to something you haven’t even built yet, and it’s exactly what allows you to avoid dead-ends and arrive closer to the mark.

This list represents the 80/20 of a Foundation of Understanding. If you’re unable to tell the story of how your customer thinks about any of the above issues, then you probably have a lot to gain by filling in that gap.

Having answered these essential basics, your Foundation of Understanding will tend to stay fairly solid, freeing you up to pursue learning goals that are more situational, strategic, and company-specific.

Don’t Ask Stuff You Could Have Googled
If a question could have been answered by googling it, then it is, by definition, an unimportant question. Get the industry research out of the way at your desk, and then use your customer conversations to learn stuff that only that customer can tell you, since it exists only within their head.

Similarly, there’s no need to keep asking different people a question that you already have the answer to. Surveys and market researchers are forced to ask the same questions every time because they need to be able to condense the resulting data into a pretty little one-pager and claim statistical significance. You, on the other hand, need to equip yourself with the customer understanding to make the next set of critical decisions.

Once you thoroughly understand some piece of your customers’ lives, it’s no longer important to keep asking about it. Use your time to ask about stuff that you don’t already know.

💡 Rule of Thumb: If the answer to a question could be googled, then it should be googled.

Continue with Specific, Strategic Unknowns
As your company and product mature, you’ll sometimes encounter an obviously important unknown.24

Not every business risk can be solved by conversation alone, and you very well might require a sales pitch, a product experiment, or a good old fashioned grind. But when the unknown can be resolved—or at least reduced—by a simple conversation, it’s almost always the most efficient use of your time to do so.25

For example, my current business26 makes tools for indie nonfiction authors, who we understand quite well. But that’s a niche-within-a-niche, and we often get requests, suggestions, or random desires to expand into adjacent markets like academic writing or genre fiction.

In terms of the product itself, supporting these extra segments seems trivial, which makes it tempting to say yes, and it kept coming up in our product roadmap meetings. We even got as far as designing out the wireframes. But then we realized that we didn’t actually understand academic or fiction authors nearly as well as we would like, which was probably worth fixing before we wasted any more time on baseless speculation.

With a couple easy conversations, we learned that:

Academic authors love to complain about their existing tools and process, but are unable, for a variety of institutional reasons, to meaningfully change how they do things. In other words, they couldn’t use our product even if they wanted to (and adjusting our product to fit their constraints would be a complete reinvention, not a minor addition).

Fiction authors have a different set of pain-points than nonfiction authors, which made our product only a “nice-to-have” and not a “must-have.” On the bright side, though, they could use an essentially identical product.

With that customer understanding in hand, it felt like a clear choice to eliminate the academic option and stop wasting our mental cycles on it, while also queuing up a quick product experiment for fiction. (And as a happy byproduct, we also had a head-start on figuring out how to adjust our marketing language to align with the fiction process and worldview.)

New startups tend to take customer learning pretty seriously, since they know what they don’t know. But once you’ve got a more established product and spend the bulk of your time making more incremental, data-driven, optimization-style improvements, it’s easy to fall out of the habit. And then, when you want to make a big move—strategic expansion, major partnership, fresh business model, new product, expanded customer segment—you end up tripping over some silly obstacle that could have been discovered—and avoided—with a simple conversation.

Once you know what you want to learn, you need to know how to steer the conversation toward actually learning it.

Getting and Staying on Topic
We’ve all been there: an hour-long conversation overflowing with incredible insights about completely the wrong thing. You wanted to talk to farmers about irrigation and drought insurance, but somehow ended up talking exclusively about their deep hatred for geese. It happens.

The solution is a quick one-two punch:

Interrupt and do a hard-reset of topic, and then…

immediately return the focus onto the customer’s life

You’ve already seen an example of this technique in [[1.3 Fixing Bad Data]] (as a way to deflect compliments), but it’s also your go-to tool for staying on important topics, so it’s worth making the technique explicit.

The second step—refocus on them—is the important one. If you interrupt and then start talking, the interruption feels extremely rude, and people will disengage. But it turns out that people don’t actually perceive it as an interruption so long as the focus stays on them.

This approach is the easiest and smoothest, where you’re anchoring back to something they said earlier that you made a note of. (You are taking good notes, right?) But you can also use the approach to open completely new topics:

“sorry to interrupt, you were saying something a minute ago I was really interested in — would you tell me more about how you...”

“hey, really sorry to interrupt, and this is a bit of a sudden change in topic, but I was wondering how you have been dealing with…”

If this feels too clunky (although I promise that people don’t mind, so long as the focus returns to them), you can smooth it out by taking a beat to place them on a pedestal:

“weird question, but I was looking through your blog before this meeting, and it sounds like you’ve been working pretty hard to find a good solution to X. Would you mind telling me how you…”

I’ve seen so many founders waste time in customer conversations by being too passive, sitting there and diligently listening to whatever the customer wants to say.

That’s wrong. The correct balance is for the conversation to remain focused on the customer, but constrained by your learning goals.

Reset and refocus is your tool for doing so. Once you give it a try and it clicks, you’ll be startled by how trivially easy it is to steer a conversation precisely where you want it to go.

Extending Early Endings
I was once sitting in a cafe when I overheard a founder doing a customer interview at the table next to me. The founder was talking about some problem, and the customer responded, “Yeah, that’s pretty much the worst part of my day.” The founder jotted something down in his notebook, and then moved on to the next question. WHAT!? It’s the worst part of their day and you’re not gonna take a minute exploring into why that is? That’s insane. You’ve got to keep them talking!

Unfortunately, people don’t really want to keep talking about themselves as much as you would like, and they’ll often try to cut themselves off and let you take your own “turn” in the conversation, as a way to keep the conversation “balanced.” You’ve got to realize that most people have spent their entire lives being repeatedly discouraged from talking at length about themselves: in a social context, talking too much about yourself is seen as rude; in a professional context, it’s career-limiting. As such, a lot of people will cut themselves short, giving brief, polite, efficient replies to your questions. But you don’t want brief, polite, and efficient; you want the whole story. You want them to go long. But if they feel too self-conscious, they’ll clam up.

The two best solutions to this self-censorship—called Mirroring and Labeling—come from Chris Voss, former lead hostage negotiator at the FBI. As Voss explains in Never Split the Difference:

It’s almost laughably simple: for the FBI, a “mirror” is when you repeat the last three words [...] of what someone has just said. Of the entirety of the FBI’s hostage negotiation skill set, mirroring is the closest one gets to a Jedi mind trick. Simple, and yet uncannily effective.

By repeating back what people say, you trigger this mirroring instinct and your counterpart will inevitably elaborate on what was just said and sustain the process of connecting.

That’s mirroring, and it works really well. Let’s return to the customer conversation I overheard in the cafe:

Customer: “[...] pretty much the worst part of my day.”

All the founder needed to do was mirror (repeat) the last few critical words:

Founder: “The worst part of your day?” (*mirror)

That’s literally it. And in pretty much every case, they’ll simply keep going. You can repeat that trick over and over whenever they’ve stopped themselves short, for as long as it proves useful.

Voss’s second technique for this situation is called Labeling, where instead of repeating their exact words, you try to summarize their perspective with a label.

Let’s say that someone has gotten a bit worked up in the conversation, and shows a moment of authentic frustration or delight—a signal you’d love to learn more about—before bottling it back up to more socially acceptable levels. You might say:

“This seems like something you care a lot about.” (labeling)

Or:

“Sounds like you’ve got a lot of personal experience with this.” (labeling)

In many cases, that label will be enough to get them going—often by correcting your description and adding some much-needed nuance to it.

Other times, they’ll respond to the label with something generic and then stop themselves again, like this:

Founder: “Sounds like you’ve spent a lot of time on this already.” (labeling)

Customer: “Yeah, been putting a ton of effort into it. It’s been a big project.” (responding with a generic summary rather than concrete stories)

Founder: “A big project?” (mirroring)

Customer: “I mean, yeah, like two weeks ago I was…” (Good Data)

So long as you’re already talking about the right topic, you’d be surprised how often you can get away with just bouncing between Mirroring and Labeling until the floodgates finally open.

Sometimes it doesn’t even need to be that complicated, and you can get there with any random little conversational poke:

“Why?” “Like what?” “And then...?” “Tell me more.” “For real?”

But if you want the very best tools for the job, use Mirroring and Labeling. Very often, the only thing standing between yourself and Good Data is you haven’t yet given the other party sufficient permission to keep talking.

Specifying Generalizations
People will often respond to your questions not with concrete examples, but with broad generalizations. When someone says the words...

“I usually”

“I always”

“I never”

“I just”

When you hear a generalization, you should do three things:

Be suspicious

Get a concrete example or story

Identify the counter-examples, edge-cases, and exceptions

Sometimes the whole generalization evaporates when you ask for a story. For example:

Them: “It’s absolutely ridiculous that it works this way, it’s a huge problem.” Generic problem statement.

You: “So how have you been trying to deal with it?” Anchor generic complaint to concrete behaviors.

Them: “Umm..” I’m supposed to be dealing with it?

But it doesn’t always go that way. Sometimes you push through the generic and find a treasure trove beneath:

You: “So how have you been trying to deal with it?”

Them: “Oh, I’ve tried everything. I downloaded X, Y, and Z, and even went so far as to hire a consultant to help me do something custom, but none of it worked because...”

So this is a highly motivated, solution-aware customer who is investing considerable time, effort, and money in finding a particular type of solution. This particular person is an incredible source of customer insights, and also exactly the sort of person most likely to become one of your very first customers.

Or they might say:

Them: “Everybody uses X or Y, but they’re garbage because of P and Q. Have you ever actually tried using them? They’re a complete disaster—10 million features and none of them work right.”

Which points in a different strategic direction (i.e., out-execute and simplify) and shows a third type of insight hidden beneath the original generalization.

A helpful shortcut is to search for counter-examples, exceptions, and edge-cases.

Imagine that you’re asking a customer about how they handle their email, and they respond with a generic claim:

Them: “I just stay at Inbox Zero.” A generic claim, implying that they’ve no problems whatsoever around email.

You: “Has there ever been a time when it got really out of control?” Scanning for possible counter-examples, edge cases, and failure points.

Them: “Oh, yeah, of course. Sometimes life gets crazy.” This tells us that counter-examples do exist, but it’s still another generic answer.

You: “Life gets crazy?” Mirroring to give them permission to keep going.

Them: “Ug, like three weeks ago, I was traveling in the middle of nowhere and the internet at the hotel totally didn’t work. It took me like 10 days to get back on track.”

In this case, we took the generic claim, “My inbox is always under control” and added the important caveat: “Except when it’s not, in which case it’s a total nightmare to recover from.” Depending on what you wanted to learn, you could now take the conversation in any number of directions from here: how they recovered from the backlog, what it “cost” them to fall so far behind, whether they changed anything afterwards, and so on.

While using generics, people describe themselves as who they want to be, not who they actually are. You need to get specific to bring out the edge cases. (And those edge cases are very often how a new product finds its toehold into the market.)

💡 Rule of Thumb: Generalizations are Bad Data, but there’s Good Data hiding behind them.

Ask the Scary Questions
The other way we end up settling for Bad Data—whether it shows up as bias, incompleteness, or unimportance—is by being too afraid to ask the scary questions.

💡 Scary Questions: an important question, the answer to which might fundamentally change your business, eliminate an exciting option, or invalidate something you had hoped to be true.

I was recently on a call with an important customer. They’d been paying for our software and had given a few hints that they might be interested in a larger, bespoke partnership, which shifted the conversation away from simple product feedback and closer to pure customer learning. It was pretty high-stakes—I’d been awake for hours the night before in anxious rumination—and the first 45 minutes had been going unreasonably well, to the point that I was starting to get a bit confused.

The customer was being vastly more enthusiastic than my mental model would have predicted, which meant that my mental model was wrong, and I needed to figure out why.

Rob: “This is going to be a weird question, but can I ask why you’re even talking to us about this? Couldn’t you pretty easily just build all of this yourself?”

Founders tend to be terrified of asking this sort of question, as if the other party will leap to their feet shouting, “Gadzooks! You’re right, you are useless to me,” and then storm out of the room. But that never actually happens. Instead, they said:

Customer: “We could build it, but we won’t because [explanation of specifically why we can be valuable to them, plus some signal about how they internally price and value this particular opportunity].”

In many cases, the fact that the question feels scary is a sign that it’s exactly what you ought to be asking.

The customer’s answer was hugely clarifying, allowing me to separate quite a lot of signal from noise and improve my mental model of their goals and behavior.

As the conversation continued, I noticed that I was, once again, confused by what they were saying. I needed to figure out whether the data was in some way faulty (biased, generic, etc.), or whether my mental model needed another update:

Rob: “Sorry to go off-topic, but I’m a bit surprised by the sorts of partnerships you’re proposing here—it seems like these would involve a ton of effort from your side, and I wouldn’t have thought you cared quite that much about this.”

This is a variant of a pretty standard enterprise sales question, wherein you force everyone to get concrete about how much time and effort will be required for the product to be bought and deployed, which rapidly reveals whether they’re serious about buying. But in this case, I’m using it for pure learning, asking the customer to explain to me why they seem willing to spend so much effort on a partnership with us.

Customer: “Oh, no, my team wouldn’t do any of the actual work. I’m just saying that if you guys wanted to set something like this up for us, we’d be extremely happy to attach our name and do some co-branded promotion.”

A-ha. Well that made a whole lot more sense.

Rather than showing how much they cared, my question revealed a massive pile of Bad Data that I’d been in serious danger of tripping over.

I used to flinch away from asking this sort of question, terrified that I might “lose the deal.” But over time, I’ve realized that if a deal can be “lost” by clarifying it, then it was never really there to begin with. In this particular case, had I not continued to push through the broad-stroke enthusiasm, it would have generated exactly the sort of false-positive Bad Data that could have caused us to make a company-killing mistake. (And it’s not like my clarification eliminated any of the legitimate options hiding beneath the hype; even after getting everyone’s motivations laid bare, there were still some pretty exciting possibilities up for grabs.)

Founders seem to think that if they’re sitting down with a potential customer, their job is to “close the deal,” at any cost. But early on, that’s simply not the case. (In fact, pushing to close deals atop a shaky foundation is most likely to end up on a local maximum with a pipeline full of zombie leads, or worse). Early on, your job is to understand. And once you do, everything important about the business—product, sales, strategy, marketing, etc.—gets so, so much easier. I didn’t go into this conversation above hoping to close a deal; I went into it hoping to better understand the person I was talking to. In my experience, once you get the understanding right, a lot of the other stuff starts to take care of itself.

# How to talk to people
Good Framing: VFWPA
The framing I use has five key elements:

Vision: You’re an entrepreneur trying to solve horrible problem X, usher in wonderful vision Y, or fix stagnant industry Z. (Don’t mention your product idea, only the vision; aim for a neutral description rather than a passionate pitch.)

Flag: Clearly establish your intentions for the conversation by mentioning what stage you’re at and, if it’s true, that you don’t have anything to sell

Weakness: Prove that you know what you need by highlighting the specific issue you’ve been struggling to figure out that they could help with. (This also reassures that you aren’t going to pitch or be annoyingly self-important, and that you aren’t a time-waster.)

Pedestal: Put them on a pedestal by mentioning how much their specific experiences, in particular, could help. (This further shows you aren’t a time-waster, since you’ve done your research, and shows how they’re able to be uniquely valuable and not just an interchangeable source of commodity knowledge)

Ask: Explicitly ask for what you want (e.g., their time, their willingness to tell you how they do things, etc.)

Hey Pete, we’re a startup trying to make desk & office rental less of a pain for new businesses (vision).

We’re still in development and aren’t trying to sell anything to you, but I was hoping you might be willing to help by sharing some of your expertise. (flag)

We’ve got a good grip on the tenant side of things, but we’ve been struggling to wrap our heads around the landlord’s perspective, specifically on X and Y (weakness).

I know you must have dealt with this quite a lot when you were running Z. (pedestal).

Would you be willing to take 20 minutes to talk me through your approach to handling this stuff? It would be a huge help to us if so. (ask).
