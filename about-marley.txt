ABOUT MARLEY
Written May 28, 2026 — from a /office-hours session + the full Themomtest project context

---

You build things before you're ready, then figure out what went wrong, then build again.
The failed edtech product wasn't a detour — it was research. You got more signal from
shipping something that sucked than from a year of hypothesizing.

You're drawn to real stakes. The pre-professional program worked because the companies
were real. ReRecruit was for your actual dad. The Mom Test interviews are with real
business owners at bike shops and meat markets, not startup founders at coffee shops.
Every time an idea drifts toward simulated value ("better questions for students"),
you eventually say "this isn't enough." That instinct is good. Trust it earlier.

You're honest in the room. "We built it and it sucked" — no hedging, no spin. That's
rarer than it sounds. Most people dress up their failures. You name them directly and
use them as data.

You're a pattern-seeker running a real system. The whole Themomtest infrastructure —
the conversation log, the running lists, the map.csv, the question bank — that's not
busywork. That's a person who believes that signal compounds over time and is building
the infrastructure to make it compound. Lucas and you are doing real discovery work.
The interviews show it: you know when something was left on the table (the WorldPay
API pain dropped cold, the prior spending on phone prospectors never followed up).
You catch your own mistakes.

Your strongest instinct is toward simplicity. When the concept gets complex, you back
up. "Gusto for teachers" landed for you in a sentence. "Something that actually helps
them but is easy" — that's a product thesis, not a vague aspiration. You gravitate
toward products that handle required, painful, unavoidable tasks and make them
disappear. That's a good filter.

You care about people in thankless roles. The teacher who wants to teach the way Matt
Breen did but doesn't know how. The SMB owner drowning in disconnected systems. The
insurance agent routing the same emails 20 times a day. You notice the gap between
what people are capable of and what their environment lets them do. That empathy is
the starting point for every good product you'll build.

You're in a transition. Doing serious consulting recruiting (McKinsey, BCG, Tesla,
Point72) while also building. That tension is real. You submitted a YC video. The
fact that you're running this Mom Test system while also managing recruiting cycles
says something about what you actually want to be doing.

One pattern worth watching: you have strong signals across your interviews — WorldPay
API pain, ERP integration cost, CNP payment processing, email routing — and you keep
moving to the next conversation before making a commitment ask. The discovery is good.
The ask is where you leave value on the table. Whatever you build next, the first move
is going back to Jacob Simms and asking for the intro to Connor.

---

The through-line: you want to build things that handle the real, unglamorous, required
work that gets in the way of people doing what they're actually good at. That's a
clear product philosophy. Now find the one where the pain is undeniable, the buyer
has a budget, and the current solution is embarrassingly bad.
