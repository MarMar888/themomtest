# Three types of cust

1. DOESN’T even perceive it as a problem worth solving, despite how irrational that might seem, since they know it’s actively costing them money

2. DOES perceive it as a problem that is theoretically worth solving, but it’s so far down their list of priorities that they can’t be bothered investing any effort into actually doing so

3. DOES perceive it as a problem, but has been unable to solve it despite spending a tremendous amount of time, effort, and resources attempting to do so

☝️Rule of thumb: early stage customer conversations are for both figuring out what a customer cares about, and also for identifying the type of customer who cares. (It’s a person-problem pair, not one or other in isolation.)

Tough pill to swallow: Without the customer understanding to guide and anchor it, pure product iteration represents a false sort of speed, a spinning of wheels that never actually translates into forward progress for the business.

The dream scenario—where it feels just unbelievably, delightfully fast—is rapid product iteration while standing atop a solid foundation of understanding.

💡 Foundation of Understanding: your total accumulated knowledge about the context around your business and product, including: how your customers think and act; how your market and industry function; and where the relevant parts of society and technology are trending. (In the vast majority of cases, the weakest piece—and therefore the most impactful to fill in—is the customer understanding.)

A lot of common anti-patterns for startup failure are just different variants of being really good at some part of the business, but without the Foundation of Understanding to anchor it to reality.

Really good at visionary ideas, but weak Foundation of Understanding? What Paul Graham calls “sitcom startup ideas,” which is the sort of idea a talented team of writers would come up with if trying to give a character on their show a “visionary startup idea” without the writers actually knowing anything about the target industry


# examples
Dropbox, for example, wasn’t solving an explicit customer problem. In fact, rather than complaining about the lack of good solutions, pretty much everyone had been telling them that online file storage was either already solved or fundamentally undesirable. But Drew and Arash understood their customers’ lives—including the petty little rationalizations, workarounds, and frustrations—well enough to come up with a billion-dollar insight: it wasn’t about the features, but the friction. They believed that, if they could get the UX frictionless enough to be literally invisible and zero-effort, then people would happily pay money for online syncing; they just didn’t want to pay time for it. That UX insight, rooted in customer understanding, would require significant technical chops. Their visionary leap required deep expertise in multiple areas, not only customer understanding.

Similarly, the Yoto music player is vastly more expensive and more limited than pretty much any other music player, and is being sold—quite successfully—to people who already have a bunch of ways to play music, one of which is literally in their pocket. But the Yoto contains the right set of trade-offs to make it a delight to parents of young kids. Again, not a solution to an explicit problem. But absolutely a product that sits atop a solid foundation of understanding.

# Super important
The important part is to avoid engaging directly with the compliment. (“Oh thank you so much, I’m really glad you think so, we’ve worked really hard on it.”) Once you’ve engaged with the compliment, you’ve revealed your ego, and the conversation will remain stuck in the realm of pure Bad Data, with them falling into the role of Person Who Graciously Gives Their Opinion, and you as the Person Who Graciously Accepts It.

Avoid this by setting Learning Goals!!

Why did that person like the idea?
How much money did they say it would save them?
How would it fit into their life compared to what they’re currently doing, and what would it replace?
What else have they tried?

You’ll have some “write-off” meetings like this, especially while you’re getting started and learning these new skills. That’s okay. So long as you can notice that it happened and avoid letting it influence your decisions, you won’t have lost anything more than one conversation’s worth of time.
But entrepreneurs in this situation have a tendency to do something really, really stupid. They treat the “beep beep beep” as if it were the treasure, writing down the idea and saying thanks for the suggestion... and then they go home without digging into what was underneath.

# On mentors and experts
A simple sanity check is:

“Sounds like you have a lot of experience [in this industry / with this issue].”

I’ve asked this question and immediately felt a bit silly for questioning someone who turned out to be a paragon of the industry

- focus on operation or strategic questions
  - “What would going this direction [do for our business / let us do]?”
Time-horizon is the other big confounding factor in advice from experts, since you’ll often find yourself receiving suggestions that are Very Good Ideas, but for later.

Questions to clarify time-horizon and importance of expert suggestions (GOOD):

“Do you think we should delay the launch to add that, or is it something we would add later?”

“If you were in our shoes, is this something you’d be prioritizing above the stuff we’re already working on?”

“What percentage of our team’s total effort would you say we should be investing into this right now?”

“Any reason we shouldn’t push back the rest of our roadmap and make this our top priority immediately?”


## Hypotheticals
The world’s most dangerous customer feedback is:

**People are notoriously bad at predicting their future behavior. They say they’ll definitely do something, and then they don’t, even when it’s really important to them (like their New Year’s resolutions). Or they say they’d never want something (like having a device in their home that listens to everything they say), and then it turns out that they absolutely do.**
In other words: hypotheticals are the result of a flubbed attempt at pre-product validation.


# its a balance
In fact, an obsession with running “100% perfect conversations” can be its own source of bias. I sometimes see founders so obsessed with avoiding Bad Data that they end up running a timid, clinical sort of conversation that gets stuck on the level of superficialities. As a result, they end up with no Bad Data, but no Good Data either.

# generalizations cont. 
When you hear a generalization, you should do three things:

Be suspicious

Get a concrete example or story

Identify the counter-examples, edge-cases, and exceptions

# counterexamples

Imagine that you’re asking a customer about how they handle their email, and they respond with a generic claim:

Them: “I just stay at Inbox Zero.” A generic claim, implying that they’ve no problems whatsoever around email.

You: “Has there ever been a time when it got really out of control?” Scanning for possible counter-examples, edge cases, and failure points.

Them: “Oh, yeah, of course. Sometimes life gets crazy.” This tells us that counter-examples do exist, but it’s still another generic answer.

You: “Life gets crazy?” Mirroring to give them permission to keep going.

Them: “Ug, like three weeks ago, I was traveling in the middle of nowhere and the internet at the hotel totally didn’t work. It took me like 10 days to get back on track.”

💡 Rule of Thumb: Generalizations are Bad Data, but there’s Good Data hiding behind them.

Too impressive (frames the conversation as a polished pitch)

Too formal (frames the conversation as a tedious survey)

If your request sounds like you want to run an interview and treat them as a research subject, nobody will even bother to respond.

The unspoken contract of every meeting is that both parties benefit.

Here’s the key insight:

People like to help entrepreneurs — but only when doing so draws on their unique expertise, insights, or experiences; and when the entrepreneur already has their shit together

People like to talk about their top-of-mind goals, problems, and interests — but only when talking to someone equally interested and knowledgeable about it


# How to talk to people
Good Framing: VFWPA
The framing I use has five key elements:

Vision: You’re an entrepreneur trying to solve horrible problem X, usher in wonderful vision Y, or fix stagnant industry Z. (Don’t mention your product idea, only the vision; aim for a neutral description rather than a passionate pitch.)

Flag: Clearly establish your intentions for the conversation by mentioning what stage you’re at and, if it’s true, that you don’t have anything to sell

Weakness: Prove that you know what you need by highlighting the specific issue you’ve been struggling to figure out that they could help with. (This also reassures that you aren’t going to pitch or be annoyingly self-important, and that you aren’t a time-waster.)

Pedestal: Put them on a pedestal by mentioning how much their specific experiences, in particular, could help. (This further shows you aren’t a time-waster, since you’ve done your research, and shows how they’re able to be uniquely valuable and not just an interchangeable source of commodity knowledge)

Ask: Explicitly ask for what you want (e.g., their time, their willingness to tell you how they do things, etc.)

Hey Pete, we’re a startup trying to make desk & office rental less of a pain for new businesses (vision).

We’re still in development and aren’t trying to sell anything to you, but I was hoping you might be willing to help by sharing some of your expertise. (flag)

We’ve got a good grip on the tenant side of things, but we’ve been struggling to wrap our heads around the landlord’s perspective, specifically on X and Y (weakness).

I know you must have dealt with this quite a lot when you were running Z. (pedestal).

Would you be willing to take 20 minutes to talk me through your approach to handling this stuff? It would be a huge help to us if so. (ask).

# the friendlies
On a piece of paper, write down the type of customer you’d most like to learn from. Then make a list of specific names (ideally of people, but if not, then of companies).
Then, re-rank that list not by importance or profitability, but by friendliness and approachability.
The benefit of friendlies is that you no longer need to justify the value equation, which makes outreach and framing much, much easier:
“Hey, I’m doing some research into X. Would you be willing to help me out by answering some questions about how you Y?”

Plus, the first few conversations will often reveal that you need to change something fundamental in your customer segment or problem-space, so why not learn those early lessons without paying the overhead of convincing some stranger to give you their time?

The example’s specificity makes it easy to disregard as an irreproducible one-off, which would be a mistake. Very often, you already have access to at least some of the people you need, but just haven’t yet learned how to look for them.

# if its hard
💡 Representative Learning Segment: A subset of your target customer who is easier for you to reach and learn from, and who is representative of the broader segment.
But you have to be honest with yourself about whether this convenient subset really is representative.

💡 Who-Where Pair: The combination of a representative learning segment plus a concrete way to find them (whether physical or digital, and whether a specific place or a path of access).

# its not just about more meetings
In any case, the long-term optimization isn’t to scale to more meetings. It’s to skip the meeting altogether—and sidestep this whole issue—by integrating customer learning into your existing business activities. Which begins by Keeping it Casual.
