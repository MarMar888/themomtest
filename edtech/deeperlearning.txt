When Professor Jal Mehta and then-doctoral candidate Sarah Fine embarked on their exploration of deeper learning in American high schools, they were initially disappointed. They’d traveled to California to visit a school known for its rich, engaging instruction — but many of the classrooms they visited were far from inspiring. If deeper learning wasn’t the norm here, they wondered, where was it?

After talking to 350 students, educators, and parents across 30 schools, they found few schools where powerful learning, in which most students were engaged and thinking critically, was happening across the board. However, they did find individual teachers who were making it happen on their own. In those classrooms, students were enthusiastically engaged, participating in challenging tasks that drew on their analytical and problem-solving skills. Mehta and Fine describe these bright spots in their new book, In Search of Deeper Learning: The Quest to Remake the American High School.

Usable Knowledge sat down with Mehta and Fine, who is now the director of the teaching apprenticeship program at the High Tech High Graduate School of Education in San Diego, to find out how other teachers can replicate the successes they uncovered — and how to prepare teachers to facilitate the kind of deeper learning we'd hope to see in every setting.

You note in In Search of Deeper Learning that high schools are often considered the “the last and most challenging frontier of education reform.” People fear that by high school, some students have just fallen too far behind. But you saw deep learning in schools where the test scores might suggest that students are working below grade-level. How can good teachers facilitate “deeper learning” in high school, even when students are behind?

Mehta: Kids often can think and discuss in more sophisticated ways than they can write, if they are behind in their skills. Teachers found ways to ask questions at the level that those kids were thinking about things, which were often quite sophisticated, rather than focusing on where they were behind.

Fine: They were trusting that adolescents, regardless of what skills or knowledge they may not yet have gained, are capable of complex thinking; that their brains are ready for it, and that they're eager for it, and they'll rise to the challenge if they're given the right support. They were adding opportunities for skill-building inside of interesting work that has consequence, and meaning, and opportunities for creativity. It’s not mutually exclusive for work to be powerful and interesting, and also building skills. Having tried to do that a bit myself, it's really hard. It takes a lot of deliberate thinking and crafting of tasks. But, where we saw it, it was just night and day.

You also focus on extracurriculars as a space where deeper learning happens. What can we learn from what happens after school?

Mehta: With adolescents in particular, we are often working against their desires to contribute, to be responsible, to be productive people. That's part of why we were so taken with the extracurricular space and the club spaces: those spaces assume that students will be leaders, students will learn from other students, that students can make real things.

One of the things great teachers were able to do was bring a logic of apprenticeship to their students, allowing students to play the whole game at the junior level, thereby enlivening the classroom experience and giving students the sense that their work mattered. It was especially hard to find math teachers who were able to do this.

Why do you think this is? What are great math teachers doing?

Fine: Pure mathematics as a field is shrinking dramatically even as fields that are math-related, like engineering, are growing. But the kind of math that students often are doing in school is often some approximation of pure mathematics, rather than applied mathematics, and that's not something that very many teachers have ever done in the world, nor that many people in the world do anymore. The more promising math lessons we saw were often ones where there was an engineering component or robotics or some kind of connection to more applied math scenarios.

How can we train more great teachers?
    
Fine: My current role is designing and running a teacher-preparation program, so I think a lot about this. I think the most important change we could make is to make sure that the structure and logic of teacher training is mirroring and giving candidates experiences of the kind of learning that we're trying to get them to create in a classroom, right? If we're trying to teach teachers to treat students as sense-makers, but we just constantly talk at them, there's a lot of dissonance there. We fast-track a lot of teachers in the U.S. and put them in classrooms very quickly. The longest preparation programs we have are usually around a year.

If you don't have an instamatic mode of training people, and then there's no systematic support from professional learning when people get to schools, then what you're going to witness is what we saw: highly variable instruction; some really great teaching and other people who would like to be doing that kind of teaching but don't quite know how. – Jal Mehta

Mehta: I think on that point — would it take longer for teachers to become deeper teachers, or whatever you want to call it? And I think the answer to that is sort of yes and no. What we witnessed was the result of a non-system. If you don't have an instamatic mode of training people, and then there's no systematic support from professional learning when people get to schools, then what you're going to witness is what we saw: highly variable instruction; some really great teaching and other people who would like to be doing that kind of teaching but don't quite know how. I think we could lessen the learning time, if we were much more intentional at apprenticing new people to really skilled people who already knew how to do the work. But it would still take time.

Fine: We are nowhere near bumping up against the limits of what we could do with novice teachers if we could actually had a system to train them well.

How to promote deeper learning in your classroom
Think of your students as apprentices. The great teachers profiled by Mehta and Fine are inducting their students into the art or science — or combination thereof — of their individual disciplines and allowing students to learn by working in that domain. Rather than taking multiple choice tests about their reading, an excellent philosophy teacher was having the students co-explore texts with him as philosophy scholars. A team of math teachers had teachers develop their own theories for questions like how many breaths it would take to blow up a balloon, making meaning like a mathematician rather than relying on predetermined formulas. Students were empowered by this logic of apprenticeship, and the expectation that they, too, could be experts, applying their knowledge as they saw fit. Fine and Mehta think about this in terms of allowing students to develop mastery (knowledge and skill); identity (connection to a subject), and creativity (using their understanding for work they find meaningful).
Focus on depth over breadth. The great teachers Fine and Mehta observed emphasized going deep into a topic rather than covering a broad range of topics. That can be easier said than done, especially for subjects like history or math, where there’s pressure to get through specific content before the end-of-year state test. Rather than rushing through lectures and worrying about falling behind, the great teachers Mehta and Fine saw “were more concerned with whether students were adopting the habits and dispositions of inquirers in their disciplines.” The researchers noted that the teachers were able to do this by distancing themselves from testing pressures. Admittedly, that was often through routes not available to all teachers, like teaching an elective, or moving to a school with a compatible philosophy. But sometimes, teachers were able to get the freedom they required for deeper learning by making a strong case to principals, often bolstered by satisfied students and parents. 
Give up some control. Rarely does deeper learning happen when a teacher spends the entire classroom lecturing from the front of the room, Fine and Mehta found. By allowing students some choice in the topics they explore and the methods they use, teachers can let students see the purpose in their learning and be more engaged.



Key Takeaways
1. Deeper Learning Integrates Mastery, Identity, and Creativity
In particular, we came to think that our own distinct vision of deep learning—not simply in school, but in life—emerges at the intersection of three virtues: mastery, identity, and creativity.

Three Virtues of Learning. Deeper learning transcends rote memorization, emphasizing the integration of mastery (knowledge and skills), identity (personal connection and relevance), and creativity (application and innovation). This holistic approach fosters a more meaningful and lasting educational experience.

Mastery, Identity, and Creativity. Mastery involves developing a deep understanding of content and the ability to transfer knowledge to new contexts. Identity connects learning to students' personal interests and values, making it more engaging and relevant. Creativity encourages students to apply their knowledge in innovative ways, fostering critical thinking and problem-solving skills.

Powerful Learning Experiences. The most impactful learning experiences occur when these three virtues intersect, creating a synergistic effect that enhances understanding, motivation, and the ability to apply knowledge in meaningful ways. This integration prepares students not only for academic success but also for the demands of twenty-first-century life.

2. The "Grammar of Schooling" Impedes Deeper Learning
Schools are actively trying to shed the long hand of the past, but have not yet arrived at the future.

Historical Constraints. The traditional "grammar of schooling," established in the late 19th century, includes age-graded classrooms, disciplinary silos, and teacher-centered pedagogy. These structures often hinder deeper learning by promoting passive learning and rote memorization.

External Pressures. External forces, such as standardized testing, district-mandated curricula, and parental expectations, further constrain deeper learning by emphasizing breadth over depth and prioritizing test scores over genuine understanding. These pressures often lead to a narrowing of the curriculum and a focus on test preparation.

Need for Systemic Change. Overcoming these impediments requires a fundamental shift in the way schools are organized and how teaching is approached, moving away from a knowledge-transmission model toward one that fosters critical thinking, engagement, and creativity. This shift necessitates addressing both structural constraints and the underlying beliefs and values that shape educational practices.

3. Project-Based Learning Fosters Creativity and Engagement
With this project, it’s about taking something from inside yourself and saying, ‘I want to get this done because I want to get this done, not I want to get this done because I want to get an A-plus,’

Hands-on, Minds-on. Project-based learning (PBL) engages students in creating original work, connecting learning to real-world applications, and fostering collaboration and problem-solving skills. This approach can be highly effective in promoting deeper learning by integrating mastery, identity, and creativity.

Dewey High's Model. Dewey High exemplifies successful PBL by integrating technical and vocational elements with a liberal arts curriculum, empowering teachers to design curricula that reflect their unique knowledge and passions, and fostering a culture of contribution and community engagement.

Potential Pitfalls. However, PBL can also fall short if it lacks rigor, clear learning goals, and opportunities for students to develop foundational skills. Confusing "hands-on" with "minds-on" can lead to superficial engagement without genuine understanding.

4. No Excuses Schools Prioritize Mastery and Equity

High Expectations and Structure. No Excuses schools emphasize strict discipline, high academic expectations, and a focus on preparing students for college. This model aims to provide disadvantaged students with the skills and knowledge needed to succeed in higher education and beyond.

No Excuses High's Approach. No Excuses High exemplifies this model by implementing a highly structured curriculum, providing extensive teacher training and support, and fostering a culture of hard work and accountability. This approach has led to impressive gains in test scores and college acceptance rates.

Potential Tradeoffs. However, the No Excuses model can also come at the cost of student engagement, creativity, and a sense of personal identity. The emphasis on control and conformity may stifle students' intrinsic motivation and limit their opportunities for self-expression.

5. International Baccalaureate Balances Breadth and Depth

Holistic Education. The International Baccalaureate (IB) program seeks to provide a well-rounded education that balances breadth and depth, encourages critical thinking, and promotes international-mindedness. The IB Diploma Programme (IBDP) requires students to engage in rigorous coursework, complete an extended essay, and participate in creativity, action, and service (CAS) projects.

IB High's Implementation. IB High exemplifies successful implementation of the IBDP by fostering a culture of inquiry, providing extensive teacher support, and emphasizing student-centered instruction. This approach has led to high levels of student engagement and achievement.

Potential Challenges. However, the IB program can also be challenging to implement effectively, particularly in schools serving disadvantaged students. Without adequate support and resources, the program may become overly focused on test preparation and fail to foster genuine intellectual curiosity and creativity.

6. Electives and Extracurriculars Offer Powerful Learning Experiences

Beyond the Core. Electives and extracurricular activities often provide opportunities for deeper learning that are lacking in core academic classes. These activities allow students to pursue their passions, develop real-world skills, and engage in collaborative projects.

Apprenticeship Model. Many extracurricular activities, such as theater, journalism, and debate, operate on an apprenticeship model, in which students learn from experienced mentors and gradually take on more responsibility. This model fosters a sense of ownership, creativity, and community.

Bridging the Gap. By infusing core academic classes with the qualities of electives and extracurriculars, schools can create more engaging and meaningful learning experiences for all students. This may involve incorporating project-based learning, providing more student choice, and fostering a culture of collaboration and creativity.

7. Deeper Teaching Requires a Shift in Stance
We’re trying to create a context where people are collaborating together to create products of lasting value, which often morph into products of use to the community.

Beyond Transmission. Deeper teaching goes beyond simply transmitting knowledge to students. It involves creating opportunities for students to think critically, solve problems, and apply their knowledge in meaningful ways.

Key Elements of Deeper Teaching:

Substantive knowledge of the subject matter
A view of the subject as open-ended and evolving
A focus on inquiry and exploration
A commitment to student empowerment
Seminal Learning Experiences. Teachers who embrace deeper teaching often have had transformative learning experiences that have shaped their understanding of their subject and their role as educators. These experiences inspire them to create similar opportunities for their students.

8. Systemic Change is Needed to Support Deeper Learning
We’re trying to create a context where people are collaborating together to create products of lasting value, which often morph into products of use to the community.

Beyond Individual Classrooms. While individual teachers can create powerful learning experiences in their classrooms, systemic change is needed to support deeper learning at scale. This requires addressing the structural constraints and cultural norms that hinder innovation and equity.

Key Areas for Systemic Change:

Curriculum design
Assessment practices
Teacher preparation and professional development
School leadership and culture
A Call to Action. By transforming these systems, we can create a more equitable and engaging educational system that prepares all students for success in the twenty-first century.
