MISTAKES WE MADE — Mom Test Analysis
Transcripts: South Lake Excelsior (Aaron) + Freewheel Eden Prairie
Date analyzed: May 12, 2026
======================================================================

CONVERSATION 1: South Lake / Excelsior (Aaron the mechanic)
------------------------------------------------------------

1. REVEALED OUR HAND EARLY
   Lucas said: "Yeah, no, we're pretty entrepreneurial and looking at a bunch of different industries."
   Problem: Once they know you're scoping an industry to potentially enter, they shift into being helpful/encouraging instead of honest. Aaron became a tour guide instead of a problem-sharer.

2. ASKED GENERIC INDUSTRY OPINION QUESTIONS
   Lucas asked: "How have bike sales been good? Like, is it still a growing industry or no?"
   Problem: Vague, future-adjacent, invites cheerleading. We got a surface-level answer about e-bikes. Should have asked about specific past instances — a bad season, a specific month that killed them, what they actually worried about.

3. NO COMMITMENT ASK AT THE END
   Lucas ended with: "I'll be stopping back by whenever."
   Problem: Classic zombie close. No next step, no intro request, nothing concrete. We learned the owner is Dan and he's in Wednesday–Friday. We should have asked for an intro to Dan on the spot.

4. TALKED ABOUT OURSELVES INSTEAD OF LISTENING
   Lucas: "I want to get more into biking for sure." / "We love outdoor sports. We grew up skiing and biking."
   Problem: Personal rapport is fine briefly, but we spent several exchanges talking about our own interests instead of steering back to their operational reality.

5. GAVE OUR OWN OPINION MID-CONVERSATION
   Lucas: "I think it's good to be in the store, though, and get your opinion on what bike to buy."
   Problem: We're not there to share opinions. Every time we talk, we lose signal.

6. NEVER ASKED ABOUT PAIN OR PROBLEMS
   We got great info about inventory mechanics (net 180, spring delivery crunch) because Aaron volunteered it — not because we asked well. We never directly asked: "What's the hardest part of your job?" or "What keeps the owner up at night?"

7. MISSED THE STAFFING/SUNDAY CHAOS THREAD
   Aaron mentioned Sundays are unpredictable chaos and the repair queue piles up. We moved on instead of digging in: "Walk me through a bad Sunday — what actually happened?" That was a real pain signal we let go.


CONVERSATION 2: Freewheel / Eden Prairie (sales floor employee)
---------------------------------------------------------------

1. PITCHED THE IDEA
   Lucas said: "Software or some sort of product to help you guys out or just trying to hear from people that know it best."
   Problem: This is the cardinal sin of the Mom Test. The moment you say "software or product," the interviewee starts performing helpfulness. Everything after this point is potentially contaminated — they're trying to help you, not tell you the truth.

2. TALKING TO THE WRONG PERSON
   The interviewee was a frontline sales employee with no visibility into ordering, P&L, or management decisions. He said explicitly he doesn't know the ins and outs of costs, and the regional director handles all inventory. We should have gotten the conversation to the decision-maker (regional director, owner) much earlier.

3. ASKED LEADING QUESTIONS
   Lucas: "Do you feel like they're pushing a lot more to drive revenue and sell a lot more?"
   Problem: The answer is almost always yes to a leading question like this. It's not real data.

4. LET THE CONVERSATION DRIFT INTO PERSONAL CHAT
   Rental properties in Dubuque, rugby, his girlfriend's teaching job, Maple Grove suburbs — we spent several minutes on pure social filler. Fine to build rapport briefly, but we never steered back to learning. The conversation just ended there.

5. NO COMMITMENT ASK
   Lucas ended with: "Hopefully we connect with Eric."
   Problem: The interviewee offered an intro to Eric Lynn (guy who built an app Trek acquired). That was a real commitment signal — but we didn't lock it in. We should have gotten his LinkedIn right then, asked him to send the intro while we were standing there, or at minimum gotten the employee's name and contact info to follow up.

6. NEVER ASKED ABOUT SPECIFIC PAST PROBLEMS
   The closest we got was asking what frustrates him day-to-day, and he gave a decent answer (checking in bikes for service at the old job, role confusion). But we never pushed for a specific story: "Tell me about the last time that was really bad — what actually happened?"

7. NO BIG 3 QUESTIONS
   No visible structure. We wandered from inventory → management structure → margin → costs → social chat. We didn't know what we were trying to learn before going in, so we couldn't steer toward it.


CROSS-CONVERSATION PATTERNS TO STOP
-------------------------------------
- We consistently fail to ask the commitment question at the end. This must become a habit.
- We reveal our intent (entrepreneurial exploration, building software) too early, which poisons honest responses.
- We accept vague answers ("yeah that's a challenge sometimes") without anchoring to a specific story.
- We never asked the destroy-your-business question: something that could prove our assumption wrong entirely.
- When someone volunteers that their current solution is inadequate ("our cameras aren't very good"), don't move on — that's the exact moment to ask "Have you ever looked at fixing that? What stopped you?" Missing this skips the have-they-searched test entirely.
- When a problem has clear emotional signal but no financial weight attached, ask "Has anyone ever tried to put a number on what this costs you?" — frustration without magnitude is hard to build on.
- When you learn of a specific, documented, recurring instance of a problem (e.g., a professional shoplifter who's been hitting multiple stores for a long time), go deeper on it: "What has the company actually tried to do about that?" Don't treat it as background detail.
- When someone mentions they have paid for a solution to a problem in the past, that is the highest-priority thread in the room — it is the only hard willingness-to-pay data available. Follow it immediately: "How much did you spend? Did it work? Why did it stop?" Letting it pass is the worst miss you can make.
- Never open with a product demo, feature explanation, or model walkthrough. Walk in with questions only. Everything the customer hears before you ask your first question contaminates the responses that follow.
- A wishlist of AI feature ideas ("I'd love it if it could prioritize my inbox, draft emails, block my calendar...") is the AI-era version of asking "would you use this?" — it is not data. Do not accept it and move on. Anchor every item to a specific past behavior: "Have you ever tried something like that before? What happened?"


CONVERSATION 3: Something Safari / Excelsior (toy store)
---------------------------------------------------------

1. NO PREPARED OPENER
   Lucas had no framing ready and got hit with "Who are you?" twice with nothing to say.
   Problem: Improvising the opener leads directly to revealing intent and pitching. The 5-part framing (who you are, what you're exploring, what you want to learn, how much time you need, is that okay?) must be memorized before walking in — not assembled on the spot.

2. APOLOGETIC UNDER FIRST FRICTION
   Lucas said: "You do not have to answer. I don't mean to take up your time."
   Problem: Signals you don't have a legitimate reason to be there. Confident curiosity would not have said this. Brief pushback is normal — collapsing at the first sign of it guarantees nothing.

3. CONTRADICTORY IDENTITY IN THE SAME CONVERSATION
   In 9 minutes: college student → entrepreneurship major → done with school → going to New York to work for Ramp.
   Problem: When the person can't figure out who you are or what you want, they stop being honest. "I'm not going to give you my secret" is the result. Decide who you are for this conversation before you walk in and do not deviate.


CONVERSATION 4: Rob Martin — Hub International (insurance / AI consulting)
---------------------------------------------------------------------------

1. OPENED WITH A PRODUCT DEMO
   Lucas opened by showing Rob the physical recording device and immediately shifted into explaining Claude Skills ("you could have the skill that knows your hub, color palette and style").
   Problem: The customer heard product features before being asked a single question. Everything Rob said after that was filtered through "how do I be helpful to these guys" rather than "what actually frustrates me." This is the same mistake as Conversation 2, scaled up.

2. EXPLAINED MODEL TIERS AND TOKEN ECONOMICS MID-SESSION
   Lucas: "I don't even know what my three levels of Claude are: Sonic, Opal, and whatever the tokens they're going to spend...if you're giving it something that needs to think deeper and reason with itself more before giving you an answer, it'll spend more tokens, which costs more money."
   Problem: Product education during a discovery conversation. It signals you are there to sell, not to learn. Rob had no use for this information and it wasted 10 minutes of the session.

3. ACCEPTED THE WISHLIST WITHOUT ANCHORING ANY OF IT
   Rob described five things he'd love AI to do: prioritize his inbox, draft email responses, block calendar time, run prospect outreach campaigns, and de-duplicate EIN records. Lucas took notes and moved on.
   Problem: None of these were anchored to a specific past story. "I'd love it if..." is aspiration, not evidence. The right response to each item is: "Have you ever tried to solve that before? What happened?"

4. LET THE PHONE PROSPECTOR SIGNAL PASS
   At the end of the conversation, someone said: "You have paid for a phone prospector in the past." The conversation moved on without a single follow-up question.
   Problem: Prior spending is the strongest willingness-to-pay signal that exists in a discovery conversation. Rob has literally paid for a solution to the prospecting problem before. Not following up — how much, did it work, why did it stop — is the biggest missed opportunity in the session.

5. NO COMMITMENT ASK
   Session ended with Rob offering to send a spreadsheet of clients and prospects (no deadline set) and saying "I'm sure I can figure out a way to pay you guys something."
   Problem: Vague future help and encouraging words are not commitments. No calendar invite, no agenda, no agreed next step. A real commitment ask would have been: "Can we schedule 30 minutes next week to go through your inbox together and build the first version of that triage workflow?"
