THINGS TO AVOID — Running List
Last updated: May 17, 2026
======================================================================

1. REVEAL INTENT EARLY
   Don't say we're "entrepreneurial and looking at industries" or that we're building software/a product. The moment they know, they shift into encouraging mode and stop being honest.

2. PITCH THE IDEA
   The cardinal sin. Saying "software or product to help you guys" poisons everything after — they start performing helpfulness instead of telling the truth.

3. ASK GENERIC OPINION QUESTIONS
   Avoid: "How have bike sales been?" or "Is it a growing industry?" These invite cheerleading and surface answers. Always anchor to a specific past event instead.

4. ACCEPT VAGUE ANSWERS WITHOUT DIGGING
   When they say "yeah that's a challenge sometimes," slow down: "Can you give me a specific example of when that happened?" Never let a general answer be the last thing you hear.

5. MISS A PAIN SIGNAL
   When they mention something chaotic or frustrating in passing (e.g. Sunday repair queue chaos), stop and dig: "Walk me through a bad version of that — what actually happened?" Don't move on.

6. TALK ABOUT OURSELVES
   Sharing personal interests or opinions burns time and changes the dynamic. Brief rapport is fine, but every exchange we spend talking is a signal we didn't get.

7. GIVE OUR OWN OPINIONS MID-CONVERSATION
   We're not there to share views. Every time we talk, we lose signal.

8. ASK LEADING QUESTIONS
   Avoid: "Do you feel like they're pushing a lot more to drive revenue?" The answer is almost always yes. It's not real data.

9. END WITHOUT A COMMITMENT ASK
   Never close with "I'll stop by sometime" or "hopefully we connect with X." Always ask for a specific next step: intro to the owner, a follow-up meeting, or a LinkedIn connection locked in on the spot.

10. TALK TO THE WRONG PERSON
    Frontline staff can't tell us about ordering, P&L, or decisions. Identify the decision-maker early and steer toward them. If the person we're talking to says "I don't know the ins and outs of that," that's the cue.

11. GO IN WITHOUT STRUCTURE
    Know what 3 things we're trying to learn before every conversation. Without that, we wander and leave with nothing actionable.

12. LET THE CONVERSATION DRIFT INTO SOCIAL FILLER
    Rental properties, sports, weekend plans — fine briefly, then steer back. If we're not learning, we're wasting the conversation.

13. NEVER ASK THE DESTROY-YOUR-BUSINESS QUESTION
    Every conversation needs: "Is this actually a problem you'd spend money to fix, or have you just accepted it?" The answer "we've accepted it" is the most valuable data we can get.

14. ASK OPINION QUESTIONS
    Avoid: "Do you think this is a good idea?" / "Would you use something that did X?" These invite polite lies. Every question should be past-tense and specific.

15. CELEBRATE COMPLIMENTS
    "That's such a great idea!" costs the customer nothing and tells us nothing. Deflect immediately: "Glad it resonates — tell me about the last time you ran into this problem." Compliments are fool's gold.

16. MIX CUSTOMER TYPES IN THE SAME SESSION
    Talking to a floor mechanic, a sales rep, and an owner in the same outing gives contradictory signals. Pick one type per batch of conversations and stick to it until patterns emerge.

17. USE "THEY SEEMED BUSY" AS AN EXIT
    "Seemed busy" is not a signal to leave — it's a constraint to work around. "I know you're slammed — two quick questions and I'm out of your hair" costs almost nothing and usually works. Backing off at the first sign of friction guarantees zero signal every time.
